Why do I keep losing winning endgames?

You traded into a simple position a piece up, and somehow didn't win it. This is the most measurable leak in club chess and almost nobody knows their own number. Rookbook works out how often you convert.

Simplifying is not the same as winning

Most players treat the trade into an endgame as the moment the game is decided. It isn't. It's the moment the game becomes technical, which is a different skill from the one that won the material in the first place.

Middlegame chess rewards seeing things: tactics, threats, the move your opponent missed. Endgame chess rewards procedure — king activity, passed pawns, opposition, knowing which trades help. Those are learnable in an afternoon and most club players never spend the afternoon, because endings feel like the boring part of a game that's already over.

An extra piece entering a simplified position should convert close to nine times in ten. If your rate is near half, the problem isn't that you lack knowledge of theoretical endings — it's that the extra piece isn't being used. Those are different diagnoses with different fixes.

The three habits that cost them

  • Shuffling instead of pushing. Being ahead makes people cautious, and cautious in an endgame means moving pieces back and forth waiting for something to happen. Nothing happens. Endings are won by advancing pawns and activating the king, both of which feel risky and are not.
  • Refusing to trade. The rule flips once you're ahead: with an extra piece you want fewer pieces on the board, not more. Every trade increases your relative advantage and shrinks the number of ways to go wrong. Players who fought hard for the material often guard it instead of spending it.
  • Arriving with no clock. Endgames need moves, and moves need time. A long think on move twelve is paid for on move fifty-five, in a position where precision is the only thing that matters. Plenty of "lost endings" were lost in the opening, on the clock.

The king is a piece

If you take one thing: in the middlegame the king hides, and in the endgame it fights. Once the queens are off, a king walking to the center is worth roughly a minor piece of activity, and the player whose king gets there first usually wins the pawn race that follows.

This single habit — noticing the moment the king should start marching — converts more endings than any amount of theoretical study. It also costs nothing to learn.

What to actually study, in order

Two endings carry nearly all the practical value:

  • King and pawn. Every simplified position eventually resolves into one, and it's the only ending where the evaluation is exact rather than approximate. Opposition and the square of the pawn are an hour's work and permanent.
  • Rook endings. They occur more often than every other piece ending combined. You don't need the whole theory — the Lucena and Philidor positions cover most of what actually appears.

Everything else can wait. Studying bishop-versus-knight subtleties before you can win king and pawn is the chess equivalent of learning a language's subjunctive before its verbs.

Measure your own rate first

You can't tell whether this is your problem from memory, because the endings you converted are unmemorable and the ones you threw away are vivid. The number is knowable, though: take the moment the board first simplifies, record what you were up, and check what happened next.

Common questions

Why do I lose endgames a piece up? Because an extra piece only wins if it's used. Shuffling instead of pushing pawns, refusing trades that simplify, and arriving with no clock account for most of it — none of which is a gap in endgame theory.

What's a good conversion rate? Entering an ending a full piece ahead should win close to nine times in ten at club level. Near half means the material isn't being converted into anything.

Which endgames should I learn first? King and pawn, then rook endings. The first is exact and underpins everything; the second happens more than all other piece endings combined.
