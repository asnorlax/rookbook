How to review your own chess games

Almost nobody reviews their games, and most of the people who do review the wrong one. The game you remember is rarely the game that cost you. Rookbook picks the ones worth your time.

Why most review doesn't work

Two things go wrong, and they compound.

The first is selection. You review the game that stung — the one where you hung a queen, or lost to someone rated 200 below you. That game is memorable, which is exactly why it's a bad sample. Memory keeps the vivid loss and quietly discards the twenty ordinary ones, so the pattern you think you're fixing is usually the pattern you happen to remember.

The second is method. Most review is: open the engine, click through, watch the evaluation bar drop, note the move it disliked. That tells you which move was best. It doesn't tell you what you should have noticed — and noticing is the part that transfers to the next game.

Pick the games your results flag

Three lists are worth more than everything else in your history:

  • Losses where you were ahead on material. These are the fastest points available to you, because you already outplayed the person. Nothing else you could study returns as much.
  • Losses decided by one moment. If your accuracy in a loss is close to your accuracy in wins, the game wasn't a gradual defeat — something specific happened. That moment is findable.
  • Games where mate was on the board. Even the ones you won. Missing a forced mate rarely costs the game, but it tells you your attention was on material rather than the king.

Notice what isn't on that list: the games you lost badly from move one. Those are usually openings you don't know or days you shouldn't have been playing, and neither is fixed by staring at the board.

Find the moment, not the mistake

A review that covers the whole game teaches you nothing, because most moves in most games are fine. You're looking for the single point where the position changed character. Three signals find it faster than clicking move by move:

  • Material swing. Where did the balance move, and was it a fair trade or a piece given away?
  • The clock. A four-minute think usually marks the moment you knew something was wrong. A run of three-second moves usually marks the moment you stopped checking.
  • Accuracy split. One bad move in an otherwise clean game shows up as a gap between your accuracy and your opponent's.

A useful test: if you can't say what should have alerted you, you haven't finished reviewing the game. Knowing the better move is not the same as knowing how you'd have found it.

Ask one question of each game

What should have alerted me?

Nearly always there's an answer, and it's concrete: a piece that stopped being defended, a knight that could reach a square nobody was watching, a back rank with no escape, an opponent's move you'd already decided to ignore before they played it.

The move is specific to that game and you will never see it again. The signal repeats every week.

Don't start with the engine

Form your own view first — where the game turned, what you'd play differently — and only then check it. What you're looking for is where you and the engine disagree, because that gap is your actual blind spot. Reading the evaluation first replaces your judgement with its judgement, which feels productive and teaches nothing.

The exception is tactics you simply didn't see. If the engine finds a forced win you'd have missed with an hour to think, note the pattern and move on. There's no insight to extract from a line you couldn't calculate.

How many, how often

Two or three games a week, properly, beats twenty skimmed in one sitting. Review is slower than playing and the returns come from depth. A single blown lead worked through carefully will change more about how you play than an evening of clicking through evaluation bars.

If you only have time for one game a week, make it one you were winning and lost.

Common questions

How should I review my own chess games? Start from your results, not your memory. Take the losses where you were ahead on material, the ones decided by a single moment, and any game where mate was available — then find the move where it turned and ask what should have alerted you.

Should I use an engine to review my games? Second, not first. Form your own view of where the game turned, then check it. The gap between your answer and the engine's is the thing worth studying.

How many games should I review? Two or three a week, properly, beats twenty skimmed. If you only have time for one, make it a game you were winning and lost.
