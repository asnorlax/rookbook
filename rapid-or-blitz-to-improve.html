Should I play rapid or blitz to improve?

Everyone gets told to play slower. It's decent advice, handed out without knowing anything about you — and your own games already answer the question. Rookbook puts your formats side by side.

What the standard advice gets right

The case for rapid is simple: you can't practice calculating if you never have time to. In a three-minute game you play what looks right, and looking right is pattern recognition — a skill you already have. Longer games are the only ones that force you to work out an unfamiliar position, which is what improves.

The second argument is quieter and stronger: slow games are reviewable. A rapid game keeps a shape you remember afterwards — an opening, a plan, the moment it turned — while blitz games blur into one you can't learn from.

Where it breaks down

The advice assumes your problem is calculation. Often it isn't. Plenty of players calculate fine and lose anyway — to the clock, to positions they were already winning. For them, "play more rapid" is a prescription for a disease they don't have.

It also ignores volume. Ten blitz games and one rapid take the same hour — but that hour holds ten openings, ten middlegames and ten endings. If your weakness is too few positions seen, it isn't wasted.

The numbers that settle it

No need to argue this in the abstract. Four numbers, read per format, tell you which camp you're in:

  • Accuracy per format. The important one. If your blitz accuracy stays close to your rapid, your chess holds up under pressure and something else is losing the games. If it falls off a cliff, you're guessing, and slower games are the fix.
  • Win rate per format. Read next to accuracy, never alone. Steady accuracy with a collapsing win rate means the clock; both falling together means your chess.
  • Seconds per move. Often nearly identical across formats — the giveaway. A player averaging eight seconds a move in both isn't playing rapid; they're playing blitz with a longer clock, handing back the advantage they paid for.
  • How much of your clock you use. If you finish rapid games with most of your time unspent, more time won't help — you aren't using what you have.

The most common finding is the least dramatic: the same seconds-per-move in every format. If that's you, the format isn't the problem — moving before you've finished thinking is, and that habit follows you into any time control.

Your ratings are not comparable

Each time control is a separate rating pool, so the same number means a different thing in each. A 900 blitz and a 900 rapid rating aren't the same strength, and the gap says more about who plays each format than about you.

Comparing formats reliably makes you feel worse for no reason. Track each against its own history: a blitz rating up forty points is progress, even if it's still two hundred below your rapid.

The honest answer

Play the format you'll actually review. That's not a dodge — it's the one factor that separates players who improve from those who just log games. A rapid game you never revisit teaches about as much as a bullet game you never revisit: nothing.

If you'll review, rapid gives you more to review per game. If you know you won't, the volume argument wins and blitz is fine — as long as you're honest that you're playing for enjoyment, not improvement. Both are legitimate; confusing one for the other is what stings.

Common questions

Should I play rapid or blitz to improve? Rapid, if your accuracy drops sharply in faster games — that's a calculation problem and only time fixes it. If your accuracy holds and only your results fall, the clock is beating you, and a longer time control just gives you more of it to waste.

Is blitz bad for my chess? Not by itself. It's a problem when it's the only thing you play, and when you never review it — which is easy to skip when a game lasts five minutes.

Can I compare my blitz and rapid ratings? No. They're separate pools with different opposition. Compare each format with its own past, not with the other.
