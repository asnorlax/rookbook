Knight forks: how to spot the tactic that wins club games

More club games are decided by a knight fork than any other tactic. It's the first one worth drilling until you see it without looking — both when it's yours and when it's coming at you. Four to solve are below.

What a fork is, and why the knight is best at it

A fork is one move that attacks two things at once. Any piece can do it, but the knight does it best, for two reasons worth understanding, not memorising.

First, the knight jumps to squares nothing else defends the same way — its eight targets are scattered in a ring, and a piece guarding one rarely guards another. Second, a knight check cannot be blocked: against a rook or bishop you can interpose, but against a knight there's nothing to put in the way. The king must move, and while it moves the knight takes whatever else it was hitting.

The royal fork

The most valuable version attacks the king and queen together — the royal fork. The mechanics are the same:

  • The knight lands on a square that checks the king and attacks the queen.
  • The check is forcing — the king must move, and can't defend the queen by stepping away.
  • Next move, the knight takes the queen — you give a knight, you get a queen.

How to actually get better at these

Two habits, in order of importance:

  • Offence: with a knight near the enemy king, ask which squares a knight's-move away also touch the queen, a rook, or a loose piece. If one does and you can reach it safely, you have a fork.
  • Defence: run the same check on your own king every move. A knight two hops from a forking square is the warning sign — don't let it arrive.

Every position above was checked by an engine: the fork is the single best move and clearly wins. That's the point of drilling clean examples — you learn the shape, then start seeing it in your own games, where nobody labels it for you.

Common questions

What is a knight fork? One knight move that attacks two enemy pieces at once. Because a knight's targets can't be blocked and are rarely defended by the same piece, the opponent usually can't save both — and when one target is the king, the check forces it.

What is a royal fork? A knight fork that hits the king and queen together. The king must move out of check, and then the knight takes the queen.

How do I stop walking into them? Before each move, look at your own king and ask whether an enemy knight could reach a square that forks it with your queen or a rook. Checking for the opponent's fork matters more than finding your own.
