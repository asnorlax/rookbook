How to find every checkmate you missed

Chess.com will show you a missed mate in a game you open. It won't tell you how many you've walked past across your whole history, or which ones actually cost you the game. This is a full guide to Rookbook's Missed Mates page — what it scans, how it counts, and how to read every column.

What it scans

Rookbook replays each game in your history one move at a time. Before every one of your moves, it tries all legal replies and checks whether any of them ends the game on the spot. If one does and you played something else, that's a miss.

Two things follow from that. The check is exhaustive — there's no guesswork about whether the mate was really there, because it plays the move and looks at the resulting position. And it runs on both sides, so the page also records mates your opponents had against you and didn't play.

No Game Review required

This is the part worth knowing before you start. Chess.com's Game Review works one game at a time, and only on games you've asked it to look at. Finding a mate in one needs no engine and no review — just the moves, which every game carries. So the scan covers your entire history, including the hundreds of games nobody has ever opened again.

That's the difference between "here's the mate you missed in this game" and "here is every mate you've missed across your history, and which of them actually cost you the result". The only figures anywhere in Rookbook that need a reviewed game are the accuracy ones, because Chess.com only publishes accuracy for games that went through review. On this page that's the Acc and Acc ± columns, which sit blank for unreviewed games; everything else is filled in for every row.

Mate in one only, on purpose

The scan looks for mate in one and nothing deeper. That's a floor, not a shortcoming. A mate in one is unarguable: no engine judgement, no "well, it was winning anyway". If you're walking past forced mates in one, deeper mates aren't the thing to work on yet. It also means the scan is fast enough to run over hundreds of games in your browser without an engine.

One oversight, not three

This is the part most naive counters get wrong. If a mate sits on the board for three of your moves and you play something else each time, that isn't three missed mates. It's one mate you didn't see, that you walked past three times.

Rookbook collapses a run like that into a single miss and records the number of moves in the Chances column. A row showing 3× is a different and worse story than three separate rows would be — the same mate stood there while you made three decisions.

The same rule applies to your opponents' misses, so "They missed" counts oversights rather than positions. Counting per position inflates both numbers, and inflates them most in exactly the games where the pattern is most obvious.

The summary strip

Across the top sit the totals for whatever games your filters currently include. They're worth understanding individually, because several answer different questions:

  • Mates found — forced mates you spotted and played, including ones you took a move or two late.
  • Mates missed — distinct oversights, using the collapsing rule above.
  • Moves walked past — total moves played while a mate sat on the board. Compare it with Mates missed: a big gap means you tend to walk past the same mate repeatedly rather than miss it once.
  • Conversion — how often you took the mate when it was there.
  • Games affected, and …and not won — the second is the one that stings: games where you missed a mate and still didn't win.
  • They missed, and …and you won — mates your opponents had against you, and how many of those games you went on to win. Completed swindles.
  • Leaked 3+ after — misses after which you went on to lose three or more points of material by the end.

Reading a row

Each row is one oversight. The columns that carry the most information:

  • Move — where it happened, as a fraction. 10/12 means the mate first appeared on move 10 of a 12-move game.
  • Chances — how many of your moves that same mate stayed available for.
  • Mate available and You played — the move that ended it, and the one you chose.
  • Cost — Stockfish's verdict on the position immediately after the move you played. A big plus means the miss was harmless and you were still crushing. Near zero or negative means that single move threw the win away.
  • Think — seconds spent on the move where you missed it. Under three seconds means you never looked.
  • Clock — time remaining when the mate first appeared. The same miss with eight minutes on the clock and with twenty seconds are different mistakes: one is blindness, the other is a scramble.
  • Mat then, Mat end, Swing — your material edge when mate was available, at the end, and what happened in between.

Think and Clock together are the diagnostic pair. A miss with a long think and a comfortable clock means you looked at the position and didn't see it — a pattern-recognition gap. A miss with two seconds and thirty on the clock isn't about pattern recognition at all.

Why Cost fills in gradually

Everything except Cost is computed instantly, because finding mate in one needs no engine. Cost does — it runs a short Stockfish search per miss to work out whether that move mattered.

So it fills in progressively while the page is open, pauses the moment you navigate elsewhere, and resumes next time you visit. Results are cached, so each position is only ever evaluated once, and the column stays blank rather than guessing if the engine isn't available. If you've just loaded a large history, leave the page open for a minute and it'll populate.

What to actually do with it

Three passes, in this order:

  • Sort by Cost and look at the misses near zero. Those are the ones that changed a result rather than delaying one.
  • Scan the Think column for anything under three seconds. That's not a chess problem, it's an attention problem, and it's the cheapest thing on this page to fix.
  • Look at Chances above 1×. A mate you walked past three times is a specific pattern your eye doesn't register — usually a back-rank or a smothered shape. Two or three of those in a row will tell you which one.

Star anything worth returning to; the ★ filter chip shows only starred games.

The caveats, plainly

Mate in one only, so a mate in three you missed won't appear. Games need move data to be scanned, which almost all Chess.com games have. And having a mate available means you were already winning, so most misses cost you moves rather than the game — the summary's "and not won" figure is the honest measure of the damage, not the raw miss count.

Common questions

How do I find checkmates I missed? Rookbook replays every game in your history and tests each of your turns for an available mate in one, then lists the position, the move you played instead, and what it cost.

Does it find longer mates? No — mate in one only. It's a deliberate floor: those are unambiguous, need no engine to confirm, and if you're missing them, deeper mates aren't the priority.

Why is the Cost column slow? It runs a short engine search per miss, only while the page is open, and caches the result. Leave the page open after a big import and it fills in.
