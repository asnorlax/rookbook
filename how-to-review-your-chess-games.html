How to review your own chess games

Almost nobody reviews their games, and most who do review the wrong one. The game you remember is rarely the game that cost you. Rookbook picks the ones worth your time.

Why most review doesn't work

The first is selection. You review the game that stung — the one where you hung a queen, or lost to someone rated 200 below you. That's why it's a bad sample: memory keeps the vivid loss and discards the twenty ordinary ones, so the pattern you think you're fixing is just the one you remember.

The second is method. Most review is: open the engine, click through, note the move it disliked. That tells you which move was best, not what you should have noticed — and noticing is what transfers to the next game.

Pick the games your results flag

Three lists are worth more than everything else in your history:

  • Losses where you were ahead on material. The fastest points available, because you already outplayed the person — nothing else returns as much.
  • Losses decided by one moment. If your loss accuracy is close to your win accuracy, the game wasn't a gradual defeat — something specific happened, and it's findable.
  • Games where mate was on the board — even the ones you won. Missing a forced mate rarely costs the game, but it tells you your attention was on material, not the king.

Notice what isn't on that list: the games you lost badly from move one. Those are openings you don't know or days you shouldn't have played, and neither is fixed by staring at the board.

Find the moment, not the mistake

A review that covers the whole game teaches nothing — most moves in most games are fine. You want the single point where the position changed character, and three signals find it faster than clicking move by move:

  • Material swing. Where did the balance move, and was it a fair trade or a piece given away?
  • The clock. A four-minute think marks the moment you knew something was wrong; a run of three-second moves, the moment you stopped checking.
  • Accuracy split. One bad move in a clean game shows up as a gap between your accuracy and your opponent's.

A useful test: if you can't say what should have alerted you, you haven't finished reviewing it. Knowing the better move is not the same as knowing how you'd have found it.

Ask one question of each game

What should have alerted me?

Nearly always there's a concrete answer: a piece that stopped being defended, a knight reaching a square nobody watched, an opponent's move you'd decided to ignore before they played it.

The move is specific to that game; you'll never see it again. The signal repeats every week.

Don't start with the engine

Form your own view first — where the game turned, what you'd play differently — then check it. You're looking for where you and the engine disagree — that gap is your blind spot. Reading the evaluation first replaces your judgment with its, which feels productive and teaches nothing. When you do want the check, Rookbook's built-in review runs the engine over every move — free, in your browser — so the second pass is one click, not another tool.

The exception is tactics you didn't see. If the engine finds a forced win you'd have missed with an hour to think, note the pattern and move on.

How many, how often

Two or three games a week, properly, beats twenty skimmed. A single blown lead worked through carefully changes more than an evening of clicking through evaluation bars.

If you only have time for one, make it a game you were winning and lost.
